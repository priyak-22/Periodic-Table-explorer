About the Periodic Table

The Periodic Table is a tabular display of all known chemical elements, arranged by increasing atomic number, electron configuration, and recurring chemical properties.

History

It was first organized in 1869 by Russian chemist Dmitri Mendeleev, who arranged elements by atomic mass and predicted new ones.

Structure

Elements are arranged in rows (periods) and columns (groups) based on similar properties. Metals are on the left, non-metals on the right.

Fun Fact

Only 94 elements occur naturally. The rest are synthetic and made in laboratories!

Introduction to the Periodic Table

The Periodic Table is like a map for all known chemical elements. It's a powerful tool that helps scientists understand how elements behave, react, and bond.

Why Was the Periodic Table Needed?

Before the table, elements were known but unorganized. Scientists wanted a way to classify elements in a way that made predictions possible. That’s where Mendeleev came in!

1869 – Mendeleev's Table

He arranged 63 known elements by increasing atomic mass and left gaps for undiscovered ones.

1913 – Henry Moseley

He discovered atomic number and gave the table its modern form, correcting mismatches in Mendeleev’s layout.

Understanding the Structure

  • Periods: Horizontal rows (1 to 7) – represent energy levels or shells.

  • Groups: Vertical columns (1 to 18) – elements share similar valence electrons.

  • Blocks: s, p, d, f blocks - based on the type of atomic orbital being filled.

What is the Periodic Law?

It states: “The properties of elements are periodic functions of their atomic numbers.” That means, if you line up elements by atomic number, similar patterns appear again and again.

How to Read the Table

Each box shows an element with its symbol, atomic number, and mass. The location tells you about the element’s size, reactivity, and electron configuration.

Fun Facts

  • Hydrogen is unique – it fits in Group 1 and 17!

  • The Lanthanides and Actinides are placed separately to keep the table compact.

  • Noble gases were discovered after Mendeleev's table and didn’t fit into his version!

  • There are still synthetic elements that don't exist naturally!

Student Tip:

Don't memorize the entire table — focus on patterns! Like how elements in the same group behave similarly. This helps in chemical reactions and equation predictions.

Periodic Trends in the Periodic Table

Periodic trends are patterns in element properties across periods and groups in the table. These trends help predict chemical behavior.

Atomic Radius

Atomic size decreases across a period (→) due to increased nuclear charge and increases down a group (↓) due to added energy levels.

← Decreases across period Increases ↓ down group

Ionization Energy

Energy required to remove an electron. It increases across a period and decreases down a group.

→ Increases across period Decreases ↓ down group

Electronegativity

Tendency of an atom to attract electrons. Increases across a period and decreases down a group. Fluorine is the most electronegative element!

→ Increases across period Decreases ↓ down group

Metallic Character

Tendency to lose electrons. Increases down a group and decreases across a period.

← Increases to left Increases ↓ down group

Understanding Periodic Table Blocks

The periodic table is divided into blocks based on the electron configuration of elements. These blocks — s, p, d, and f — help group elements with similar properties.

s-block

Includes Group 1 & 2 ,alkali and alkaline earth metals.
Electron fills in f orbital
Mostly radioactive (Actinides)
Used in high-tech industries
Ex: Uranium, Cerium

p-block

Groups 13 to 18. Contains metals, nonmetals, metalloids, and noble gases. Electrons fill the p orbital. Contains metals, metalloids, and non-metals
Electron fills in p orbital
Includes Noble gases and Halogens
Ex: Carbon, Oxygen, Fluorine

d-block

Transition metals. Found in the center of the table (Groups 3–12). Their d orbitals are being filled. Hard, dense metals with high melting points
Electron fills in d orbital
Often show variable oxidation states
Ex: Iron, Copper, Zinc

f-block

Lanthanides and actinides. These elements fill the f orbitals and are placed separately at the bottom. Electron fills in f orbital
Mostly radioactive (Actinides)
Used in high-tech industries
Ex: Uranium, Cerium

Discovery Timeline of Periodic Table Blocks

1735 - 1807

s-block

Elements like Hydrogen, Lithium, and Sodium were among the earliest discovered due to their high reactivity and abundance.

1808 - 1900

p-block

Non-metals and noble gases like Oxygen, Nitrogen, and Neon were isolated and identified during this time.

1790s - 1920s

d-block

Transition metals like Iron, Copper, and Zinc were known since ancient times, but formally studied during this period.

1900 - 1940+

f-block

Lanthanides and actinides like Uranium and Plutonium were discovered during the 20th century, often with complex isolation processes.

Electronic Configuration & Orbitals

Electronic configuration describes how electrons are arranged in an atom's orbitals. This determines an element's chemical behavior and position in the periodic table.

Rules for Filling Orbitals

  • Aufbau Principle: Electrons occupy the lowest energy orbitals first.

  • Pauli Exclusion Principle: No two electrons can have the same set of quantum numbers.

  • Hund's Rule: Electrons occupy orbitals singly before pairing up.

Aufbau Order Diagram

Examples of Configurations

  • Hydrogen (1): 1s¹
  • Carbon (6): 1s² 2s² 2p²
  • Iron (26): [Ar] 4s² 3d⁶
  • Uranium (92): [Rn] 7s² 5f⁴ 6d¹

Subshells and Orbitals

Subshells are divisions of electron shells, each containing orbitals where electrons reside. The main subshells are s, p, d, and f.

Max Electrons: 14
