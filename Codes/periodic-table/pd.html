Hydrogen (H) – Atomic Number 1

Hydrogen is a chemical element with atomic number 1 and symbol H. Its atomic mass is approximately 1.008. The electron configuration is 1s1 and it belongs to Group 1 and Period 1 of the periodic table, placed in the s-block. At standard temperature and pressure, hydrogen exists as a gas and has an extremely low density of 0.00008988 g/cm³. It has a melting point of 14.01 K and a boiling point of 20.28 K.

Hydrogen shows oxidation states of +1, and -1 in hydrides. Its electronegativity on the Pauling scale is 2.20, while the first ionization energy is 1312 kJ/mol. The atomic radius is approximately 53 pm. The most common isotopes include ¹H (protium), ²H (deuterium), and ³H (tritium), where tritium is radioactive. Hydrogen is generally a colorless gas in appearance.

This element was discovered in 1766 and is credited to Henry Cavendish who recognized it as a distinct element. Hydrogen is the most abundant element in the universe, present in stars, gas giants, water, and various organic compounds.

Important industrial uses of hydrogen include production of ammonia through the Haber process, applications in rocket fuel and fuel cells, and hydrogenation in food and chemical industries.

Physically, hydrogen has the lowest density of all gases and is colorless, odorless, and tasteless. Chemically, it is highly reactive and forms compounds with most elements. Under normal conditions, hydrogen exists as a diatomic molecule, H₂.

Hydrogen is extremely flammable and forms explosive mixtures with air. Leaks can collect in closed areas, creating a severe safety hazard.

Helium (He) – Atomic Number 2

Helium is a chemical element with the symbol He and atomic number 2. It has an atomic mass of about 4.0026. The electron configuration is 1s2 and it belongs to Group 18 and Period 1 of the periodic table. Helium is placed in the s-block, although it is a noble gas. At standard temperature and pressure, helium exists as a colorless, odorless gas. It has an extremely low boiling point of 4.22 K and melts at 0.95 K only under pressure.

Helium has an oxidation state of 0, due to its completely filled outer shell, making it chemically inert. Its electronegativity value is practically zero and its ionization energy is very high at about 2372 kJ/mol. The atomic radius is approximately 31 pm. The most common isotopes are ⁴He, which is stable, and ³He, which is rare. Helium is the second most abundant element in the universe, found mainly in stars and interstellar space.

Helium was first identified in the solar spectrum in 1868 by Pierre Janssen and Norman Lockyer, before it was discovered on Earth. Commercially, helium is extracted from natural gas deposits where it accumulates from radioactive decay of heavier elements.

Major uses of helium include filling balloons and airships, providing cryogenic cooling for superconducting magnets, especially in MRI machines, and serving as an inert atmosphere for welding and scientific experiments.

Physically, helium has the lowest boiling point of all elements and does not solidify at atmospheric pressure. Chemically, helium is extremely stable, non-reactive, and forms no stable compounds under ordinary conditions.

Helium is non-toxic and non-flammable, but inhaling helium in large amounts can displace oxygen and may cause suffocation in enclosed spaces.

Lithium (Li) – Atomic Number 3

Lithium is a chemical element with the symbol Li and atomic number 3. Its atomic mass is about 6.94. The electron configuration is 1s2 2s1 and it belongs to Group 1 and Period 2 of the periodic table. Lithium is placed in the s-block and is classified as an alkali metal. At standard temperature and pressure, lithium appears as a soft, silvery metal with relatively low density. It melts at around 453.65 K and boils at about 1603 K.

Lithium generally exhibits an oxidation state of +1 due to the loss of its single valence electron. Its electronegativity on the Pauling scale is about 0.98 and its first ionization energy is around 520 kJ/mol. The atomic radius is roughly 167 pm. The most common isotopes are ⁶Li and ⁷Li, with ⁷Li being the most abundant. Lithium occurs mainly in minerals such as spodumene, petalite and in brines found in salt flats.

Lithium was discovered by Johan August Arfwedson in 1817. The name comes from the Greek word lithos, meaning stone, because it was first identified in mineral form rather than in plants or animals. Today, most lithium production comes from brine extraction and hard-rock mining.

Lithium has wide industrial applications, especially in rechargeable lithium-ion batteries used in electronics, electric vehicles and energy storage systems. It is also used in heat-resistant glass, ceramics, lubricating greases and certain pharmaceutical treatments such as mood stabilizers.

Physically, lithium is the lightest metal and has a very low density, allowing it to float on water. Chemically, it is highly reactive, especially with water and air, forming lithium hydroxide and lithium oxide. It should be stored under oil or in inert conditions to prevent rapid oxidation.

Lithium can be hazardous due to its strong reactivity. Contact with water can lead to fire or explosion, and lithium compounds should be handled carefully with proper safety precautions.

Beryllium (Be) – Atomic Number 4

Beryllium is a chemical element with the symbol Be and atomic number 4. Its atomic mass is about 9.0122. The electron configuration is 1s2 2s2 and it belongs to Group 2 and Period 2 of the periodic table. Beryllium is placed in the s-block and is classified as an alkaline earth metal. At standard conditions, beryllium is a hard, steel-gray metal with low density and a high melting point of about 1560 K. Its boiling point is approximately 2742 K.

Beryllium commonly exhibits an oxidation state of +2, as it loses both of its 2s electrons. Its electronegativity on the Pauling scale is around 1.57 and its first ionization energy is about 900 kJ/mol. The atomic radius is approximately 112 pm. The stable isotope ⁹Be is the most abundant form. Beryllium occurs primarily in minerals such as beryl and bertrandite, and commercial extraction usually comes from these ores.

Beryllium was discovered by Louis Nicolas Vauquelin in 1797 while analyzing beryl gemstones. It was isolated in pure metallic form later in the nineteenth century. The name comes from the mineral beryl, which contains beryllium in significant amounts.

Beryllium is used in aerospace and satellite components due to its high strength-to-weight ratio, as well as in X-ray windows, nuclear reactors as a neutron moderator, and various precision instruments. Its alloys, especially beryllium-copper, are valued for their strength, electrical conductivity and resistance to fatigue.

Physically, beryllium is lightweight, strong and has a high melting point. Chemically, it is less reactive than other alkaline earth metals and forms a thin protective oxide layer that prevents rapid oxidation. It reacts slowly with acids to form beryllium salts.

Beryllium and its compounds are toxic and must be handled with strict safety precautions. Inhalation of beryllium dust can cause serious lung disease known as chronic beryllium disease, so industrial use requires proper protective measures.

Boron (B) – Atomic Number 5

Boron is a chemical element with the symbol B and atomic number 5. Its atomic mass is about 10.81. The electron configuration is 1s2 2s2 2p1 and it belongs to Group 13 and Period 2 of the periodic table. Boron is placed in the p-block and is classified as a metalloid. In its pure form, boron is a hard, black, brittle solid with a high melting point of around 2349 K and a boiling point of approximately 4200 K.

Boron commonly exhibits an oxidation state of +3. Its electronegativity on the Pauling scale is about 2.04, and its first ionization energy is roughly 801 kJ/mol. The atomic radius is about 87 pm. The most abundant isotopes are ¹¹B and ¹⁰B. Boron is not found free in nature; instead, it occurs in compounds such as borates, borax and colemanite, which are important commercial sources.

Boron was first recognized in the early nineteenth century. It was independently isolated by Joseph-Louis Gay-Lussac, Louis Jacques Thénard and by Humphry Davy in 1808. The name is derived from “borax,” one of its principal compounds historically used in glassmaking and cleaning.

Boron has many important applications. It is used in borosilicate glass (heat-resistant glassware), detergents, semiconductors, fiber-reinforced composites, and boron carbide for armor materials. Boron compounds are also used in agriculture as micronutrients and in nuclear reactors because certain isotopes are strong neutron absorbers.

Physically, boron is lightweight, very hard and has a high melting point. Chemically, it forms covalent compounds and often behaves more like a nonmetal, showing strong electron-sharing tendencies. Boron forms borates, borides and various boron-hydrogen compounds known as boranes.

Boron compounds are generally considered safe in small amounts, but high exposure can be harmful. Fine boron powders and industrial dusts should be handled with proper protection, especially in manufacturing environments.

Carbon (C) – Atomic Number 6

Carbon is a chemical element with the symbol C and atomic number 6. Its average atomic mass is about 12.011. The electron configuration is 1s2 2s2 2p2 and it belongs to Group 14 and Period 2 of the periodic table. Carbon is placed in the p-block and is classified as a nonmetal. Carbon occurs in several allotropic forms, including graphite, diamond, graphene and fullerenes. It has a sublimation point of about 3915 K.

Carbon commonly exhibits oxidation states ranging from −4 to +4, depending on the compounds it forms. Its electronegativity on the Pauling scale is about 2.55, and its first ionization energy is around 1086 kJ/mol. The covalent atomic radius is approximately 77 pm. The most common isotopes are ¹²C and ¹³C, which are stable, and ¹⁴C, which is radioactive and used in radiocarbon dating. Carbon is widely distributed in nature and is found in minerals, fossil fuels, the atmosphere and all living organisms.

Carbon has been known since ancient times in the form of charcoal and soot. Graphite and diamond have long been used as tools, pigments and gemstones. Modern discoveries of new carbon allotropes, such as graphene and carbon nanotubes, have opened advanced technological fields.

Carbon has enormous industrial and biological importance. It forms the backbone of organic chemistry and is essential to life. It is used in steel production, petroleum refining, polymers, batteries, electronics, filters, activated charcoal, and advanced materials like carbon fiber and nanotubes.

Physically, carbon shows very different properties depending on its form. Diamond is extremely hard and transparent, while graphite is soft, black and conducts electricity. Chemically, carbon forms strong covalent bonds, long chains and complex molecules, which gives rise to millions of organic compounds.

Carbon itself is generally stable, but some carbon compounds can be toxic or reactive. Carbon monoxide, for example, is dangerous because it binds strongly to hemoglobin. Proper ventilation and handling are important when dealing with combustion products and industrial carbon compounds.

Nitrogen (N) – Atomic Number 7

Nitrogen is a chemical element with the symbol N and atomic number 7. Its atomic mass is about 14.007. The electron configuration is 1s2 2s2 2p3 and it belongs to Group 15 and Period 2 of the periodic table. Nitrogen is placed in the p-block and is classified as a nonmetal. At standard temperature and pressure, nitrogen exists as a colorless, odorless diatomic gas (N₂), which makes up about 78 percent of Earth’s atmosphere. It has a boiling point of 77.36 K and a melting point of 63.15 K.

Nitrogen can exhibit a wide range of oxidation states from −3 to +5, depending on its compounds. Its electronegativity on the Pauling scale is about 3.04, and its first ionization energy is roughly 1402 kJ/mol. The atomic radius is around 75 pm. The most common isotopes are ¹⁴N and ¹⁵N. Nitrogen occurs naturally in the atmosphere and in compounds such as nitrates, ammonia and organic molecules.

Nitrogen gas was first isolated in the late eighteenth century. It was studied by several scientists, including Daniel Rutherford in 1772, who is often credited with its discovery. Over time, nitrogen was recognized as an essential component of living organisms and as part of the global nitrogen cycle.

Nitrogen has many industrial uses. It is essential for producing ammonia (Haber process), which is used in fertilizers, and it plays a key role in explosives, pharmaceuticals and synthetic materials. Liquid nitrogen is used for cryogenic cooling, food preservation, and scientific research. Nitrogen gas is also used as an inert atmosphere in welding and manufacturing.

Physically, nitrogen is a low-boiling, inert gas. Chemically, the N≡N triple bond in molecular nitrogen is very strong, making N₂ relatively unreactive under normal conditions. However, nitrogen forms many important compounds, including ammonia, nitrates and organic amines.

Nitrogen gas is generally safe, but it can displace oxygen in enclosed spaces and cause suffocation. Some nitrogen compounds, such as nitrites and certain nitrogen oxides, are toxic or environmentally harmful and should be handled carefully.

Oxygen (O) – Atomic Number 8

Oxygen is a chemical element with the symbol O and atomic number 8. Its atomic mass is about 15.999. The electron configuration is 1s2 2s2 2p4 and it belongs to Group 16 and Period 2 of the periodic table. Oxygen is placed in the p-block and is classified as a nonmetal. At standard temperature and pressure, oxygen exists mainly as a colorless, odorless diatomic gas (O₂). It has a boiling point of 90.19 K and a melting point of 54.36 K.

Oxygen commonly exhibits oxidation states of −2 in most compounds, although it can show other states such as −1, +1, +2 in certain compounds like peroxides or with fluorine. Its electronegativity on the Pauling scale is about 3.44, and its first ionization energy is roughly 1314 kJ/mol. The atomic radius is around 73 pm. The most common isotopes are ¹⁶O, ¹⁷O and ¹⁸O, with ¹⁶O being the most abundant. Oxygen is widely distributed in Earth’s crust, oceans and atmosphere, and is essential for life.

Oxygen was independently discovered by Carl Wilhelm Scheele and Joseph Priestley in the 1770s. The name “oxygen” comes from the Greek words meaning “acid former,” because early chemists believed oxygen was necessary in all acids, a view later corrected with modern chemical understanding.

Oxygen has many critical uses. It is necessary for respiration, combustion, metallurgy and chemical synthesis. Liquid oxygen is used as an oxidizer in rocket propulsion, while industrial oxygen is used in steelmaking, medical oxygen therapy and water treatment. Ozone (O₃), an allotrope of oxygen, plays a vital role in absorbing harmful ultraviolet radiation in the upper atmosphere.

Physically, oxygen is a reactive nonmetal and supports combustion. Chemically, it forms oxides with most elements and participates in many biological and geochemical cycles, including respiration and photosynthesis. It readily forms compounds such as water, carbon dioxide and various minerals.

Oxygen is essential to life, but high concentrations can increase fire risk. Liquid oxygen is extremely cold and can cause severe burns. Proper handling and storage are important in industrial and medical settings.

Fluorine (F) – Atomic Number 9

Fluorine is a chemical element with the symbol F and atomic number 9. Its atomic mass is about 18.998. The electron configuration is 1s2 2s2 2p5 and it belongs to Group 17 and Period 2 of the periodic table. Fluorine is placed in the p-block and is classified as a halogen. At standard temperature and pressure, fluorine is a pale yellow gas with a boiling point of 85.03 K and a melting point of 53.53 K.

Fluorine almost always exhibits an oxidation state of −1 in compounds. It is the most electronegative element with a Pauling value of about 3.98, and its first ionization energy is roughly 1681 kJ/mol. The atomic radius is around 71 pm. Its only stable isotope is ¹⁹F. Fluorine is not found free in nature because of its extreme reactivity, but it occurs in minerals such as fluorspar (calcium fluoride) and cryolite.

Fluorine was isolated by Henri Moissan in 1886, who developed a method to electrolyze hydrogen fluoride. His achievement was significant because fluorine’s reactivity made it difficult and dangerous to obtain in pure form.

Fluorine has important industrial uses. It is used to produce fluorocarbons, refrigerants, and polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE), known as Teflon. Fluoride compounds are added to toothpaste and some water supplies to help prevent dental cavities. Uranium hexafluoride, a fluorine compound, is essential in the nuclear fuel cycle for uranium enrichment.

Physically, fluorine is a light, highly reactive gas with a sharp odor. Chemically, it is the most reactive of all elements and forms compounds with almost every other element, including some noble gases under special conditions. It vigorously reacts with many substances, often producing heat and sometimes flames.

Fluorine and many fluoride compounds are dangerous and corrosive. Direct exposure to fluorine gas can cause severe burns, respiratory damage, and chemical injuries. Industrial handling requires strict safety controls, protective equipment and proper ventilation.

Neon (Ne) – Atomic Number 10

Neon is a chemical element with the symbol Ne and atomic number 10. Its atomic mass is about 20.180. The electron configuration is 1s2 2s2 2p6 and it belongs to Group 18 and Period 2 of the periodic table. Neon is placed in the p-block and is classified as a noble gas. At standard temperature and pressure, neon exists as a colorless, odorless gas with a very low density. It liquefies at 24.56 K and boils at 27.07 K.

Neon has an oxidation state of 0 under normal conditions because its outer shell is complete, making it extremely chemically inert. Its Pauling electronegativity is undefined (because it does not form stable compounds under ordinary conditions), and its first ionization energy is around 2081 kJ/mol. The atomic radius is about 38 pm. The most stable and common isotopes are ²⁰Ne, ²¹Ne, and ²²Ne. Neon is relatively rare in Earth’s atmosphere, composing a small fraction of the air; it is more abundant in stars and interstellar space.

Neon was discovered in 1898 by Sir William Ramsay and Morris Travers during the study of liquefied air. Its name comes from the Greek word neos, meaning “new,” because it was a newly identified component of the atmosphere at that time.

Neon has several important uses. It is widely used in neon lighting and signage, producing bright red-orange glow in discharge tubes that makes it popular for advertising signs. Neon is also used in certain types of high-voltage indicators, vacuum tubes, and as a component in specialized cryogenic refrigeration mixtures.

Physically, neon is a very light gas with low boiling and melting points, and does not react under ordinary conditions because of its stable electron configuration. Chemically, neon is considered inert and forms virtually no stable compounds at room temperature or pressure. Its inertness and stability make neon safe in normal handling.

Neon is non-toxic, non-flammable, and chemically inert. Because it does not easily react or combine with other elements, it poses minimal chemical hazard under normal conditions. However, as with any gas in enclosed spaces, extreme concentrations could displace oxygen and should be avoided.

Sodium (Na) – Atomic Number 11

Sodium is a chemical element with the symbol Na and atomic number 11. Its atomic mass is about 22.990. The electron configuration is 1s2 2s2 2p6 3s1 and it belongs to Group 1 and Period 3 of the periodic table. Sodium is placed in the s-block and is classified as an alkali metal. At standard conditions, sodium is a soft, silvery metal that can be cut easily with a knife. It melts at around 370.87 K and boils at about 1156 K.

Sodium usually shows an oxidation state of +1 in its compounds. Its Pauling electronegativity is about 0.93, and its first ionization energy is roughly 496 kJ/mol. The atomic radius is around 186 pm. The most abundant stable isotope is ²³Na. Sodium does not occur free in nature because it reacts quickly with air and water, forming compounds such as common salt (sodium chloride) and sodium carbonate in minerals and seawater.

Sodium was first isolated by Sir Humphry Davy in 1807 using electrolysis of molten sodium hydroxide. This was an important step in the early development of electrochemistry and the isolation of several reactive metals.

Sodium has many important uses. It is a key component of table salt and plays a vital role in biological processes such as nerve conduction and fluid balance in living organisms. Industrially, sodium compounds are used in the manufacture of glass, paper, soaps, and detergents. Metallic sodium is used in certain sodium-vapor lamps that produce a strong yellow light for street lighting, and in some chemical synthesis processes.

Physically, sodium is soft and lightweight. Chemically, it is highly reactive and quickly forms a layer of oxide when exposed to air. It reacts vigorously with water, producing sodium hydroxide and hydrogen gas, often with heat and sometimes flames. Because of its reactivity, sodium is stored in substances like oil or kerosene to prevent contact with air or moisture.

Sodium metal is considered reactive and hazardous. Direct contact with water can cause violent reactions. In the body, excessive intake of sodium compounds like sodium chloride can contribute to health issues such as high blood pressure, although sodium is essential in small, balanced quantities. Handling metallic sodium requires protective equipment and proper storage.
