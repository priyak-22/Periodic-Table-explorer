Complete Timeline of the Periodic Table

1669

First modern elemental discovery

Hennig Brand isolates phosphorus, marking one of the earliest discoveries of an element by chemical means.

1766

Hydrogen identified

Henry Cavendish studies “inflammable air,” later named hydrogen by Antoine Lavoisier, paving the way for systematic elemental understanding.

1789

Lavoisier’s list of elements

Antoine Lavoisier publishes a list distinguishing elements from compounds, establishing foundational chemical taxonomy.

1803–1808

Dalton’s atomic theory

John Dalton proposes atoms as indivisible units and introduces relative atomic weights, enabling quantitative comparisons between elements.

1817–1829

Early periodic patterns

Johann Döbereiner identifies “triads”—groups of three elements with related properties—hinting at periodic relationships.

1857–1864

Valence and periodicity concepts evolve

Frankland’s valence ideas and Newlands’ Law of Octaves suggest repeating chemical properties when elements are ordered by atomic weight.

1869

Mendeleev’s periodic table published

Dmitri Mendeleev arranges elements by atomic weight and properties, predicts undiscovered elements, and leaves gaps for future discoveries.

1894–1898

Noble gases discovered

Rayleigh and Ramsay identify argon and other noble gases, leading to the addition of a new group to the periodic table.

1897

Electron discovered

J. J. Thomson’s discovery of the electron begins a deeper understanding of atomic structure underlying periodic trends.

1909–1911

Nuclear model of the atom

Rutherford’s gold foil experiment reveals a dense nucleus, reshaping atomic models and the theoretical basis of periodicity.

1913

Moseley and atomic number

Henry Moseley shows that atomic number, not atomic weight, orders elements correctly, correcting inconsistencies in Mendeleev’s table.

1913–1920s

Quantum foundations

Bohr’s model and emerging quantum theory explain electron shells and periodic trends, giving theoretical support to the table’s structure.

1930s–1940s

Artificial elements and transuranium

Particle accelerators enable synthesis of elements beyond uranium; neptunium and plutonium broaden the table into new territory.

1945–1955

Actinide concept

Glenn T. Seaborg reorganizes the table by placing actinides below lanthanides, clarifying f-block placement and chemical behavior.

1960s–1980s

Standardization and pedagogy

International bodies promote consistent group numbering and layouts; periodic trends become central in chemical education worldwide.

1994–2010

Elements 110–118 pursued

Collaborations synthesize superheavy elements, extending the table and testing theories about nuclear stability and the “island of stability.”

2016

Nh, Fl, Mc, Ts named

IUPAC approves names for elements 113 (Nihonium), 114 (Flerovium), 115 (Moscovium), 117 (Tennessine), and 118 (Oganesson), completing period 7.

2019

International Year of the Periodic Table

Global recognition of the table’s 150th anniversary sparks new educational resources and public engagement with chemistry.

2020s

Ongoing research

Experiments continue on superheavy elements and periodicity at extremes, refining atomic data, isotopic masses, and boundaries of the table.
