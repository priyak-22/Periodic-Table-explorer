Hydrogen (H)

Hydrogen is the lightest and simplest chemical element in the periodic table. It has an atomic number of 1, meaning it contains only one proton and one electron. Due to its simple structure, hydrogen is highly reactive and rarely exists in its free form on Earth.

Hydrogen is the most abundant element in the universe and plays a vital role in the life cycle of stars. In stellar cores, hydrogen atoms undergo nuclear fusion to form helium, releasing enormous amounts of energy.

On Earth, hydrogen is commonly found combined with other elements, such as oxygen in water and carbon in organic compounds. It is widely used in fuel cells, petroleum refining, ammonia production, and space rocket propulsion.

Helium (He)

Helium is a colorless, odorless, and non-reactive noble gas with an atomic number of 2. It is the second lightest element in the periodic table and exists as a monoatomic gas under normal conditions.

Helium is formed in large quantities through nuclear fusion in stars and is one of the most abundant elements in the universe. Due to its very low density, helium can escape Earth’s gravity, making it relatively rare on our planet.

On Earth, helium is mainly obtained from natural gas deposits. It is widely used in cryogenics for cooling superconducting magnets, in balloons and airships, in deep-sea diving mixtures, and in leak detection systems due to its inert nature.

Lithium (Li)

Lithium is a soft, silvery-white alkali metal with an atomic number of 3. It is the lightest metal in the periodic table and is highly reactive, especially with water and air, so it is usually stored in oil.

Lithium is not found freely in nature due to its reactivity. It occurs mainly in minerals such as spodumene and lepidolite, and in trace amounts in seawater. It was formed during the early stages of the universe and through certain stellar processes.

Lithium is widely used in rechargeable lithium-ion batteries, making it essential for modern electronics and electric vehicles. It is also used in heat-resistant glass and ceramics, in lubricating greases, and in medicine for treating certain mental health conditions.

Beryllium (Be)

Beryllium is a hard, grayish-white alkaline earth metal with an atomic number of 4. It is lightweight, strong, and brittle, and has a high melting point compared to many other light metals.

Beryllium does not occur freely in nature. It is mainly found in minerals such as beryl and bertrandite. The element was formed during cosmic processes and is relatively rare in Earth’s crust.

Beryllium is widely used in aerospace and defense industries because of its high strength-to-weight ratio. It is also used in X-ray equipment, nuclear reactors, and precision instruments. However, beryllium compounds are toxic, so they must be handled with great care.

Boron (B)

Boron is a hard, black, and brittle metalloid with an atomic number of 5. It has properties that are intermediate between metals and non-metals and exists in several allotropic forms.

Boron does not occur freely in nature. It is found mainly in compounds such as borax and kernite, which are formed through the evaporation of saline lakes. Boron is relatively rare in Earth’s crust.

Boron is widely used in the manufacture of borosilicate glass, detergents, and fiberglass. It is also important in agriculture as a micronutrient for plant growth and is used in nuclear reactors to control neutron reactions.

Carbon (C)

Carbon is a non-metallic element with an atomic number of 6 and is the basis of all known life on Earth. It exists in several allotropes, including diamond, graphite, and amorphous carbon, each with very different physical properties.

Carbon is widely distributed in nature and is found in the atmosphere as carbon dioxide, in living organisms, and in fossil fuels such as coal, oil, and natural gas. It is formed in stars through nuclear fusion and is one of the most abundant elements in the universe.

Carbon is essential in organic chemistry and is used in fuels, steel production, and filtration systems. Its compounds are used in plastics, medicines, and countless industrial and biological processes.

Nitrogen (N)

Nitrogen is a colorless, odorless, and non-metallic gas with an atomic number of 7. It makes up about 78 percent of Earth’s atmosphere and exists as a diatomic molecule under normal conditions.

Nitrogen is essential for life and is a key component of proteins and nucleic acids. Although it is abundant in the atmosphere, most living organisms cannot use it directly and depend on nitrogen fixation by bacteria or industrial processes.

Nitrogen is widely used in the production of fertilizers, which support modern agriculture. It is also used in the manufacture of explosives, in food preservation, and as a protective inert gas in various industrial applications.

Neon (Ne)

Neon is a colorless, odorless, and chemically inert noble gas with an atomic number of 10. It exists as a monoatomic gas and is known for its bright reddish-orange glow when used in electric discharge tubes.

Neon is formed in stars through nuclear fusion processes and is one of the most abundant elements in the universe. On Earth, it is present in very small amounts in the atmosphere and is obtained by the fractional distillation of liquid air.

Neon is widely used in advertising signs, indicator lamps, and high-voltage testers because of its distinctive glow. It is also used in cryogenic refrigeration and in certain types of lasers due to its stability and low boiling point.

Sodium (Na)

Sodium is a soft, silvery-white alkali metal with an atomic number of 11. It is highly reactive and readily reacts with water and air, so it is usually stored under oil to prevent unwanted reactions.

Sodium does not occur freely in nature due to its high reactivity. It is commonly found in compounds such as sodium chloride in seawater and rock salt. Sodium is formed in stars through nuclear processes and is relatively abundant in Earth’s crust.

Sodium is widely used in the chemical industry for the production of soaps, detergents, and glass. It is also essential for living organisms, playing a key role in nerve impulse transmission and fluid balance in the human body.

Magnesium (Mg)

Magnesium is a shiny, silvery-white alkaline earth metal with an atomic number of 12. It is lightweight and moderately reactive, and it burns with a bright white flame when ignited.

Magnesium does not occur freely in nature. It is commonly found in minerals such as magnesite and dolomite, and in dissolved form in seawater. The element is produced in stars and is one of the abundant elements in Earth’s crust.

Magnesium is widely used in lightweight alloys for automobiles and aircraft. It is also important in the production of fireworks and flares, in metallurgy, and as an essential element for living organisms, playing a key role in muscle and nerve function.

Aluminium (Al)

Aluminium is a lightweight, silvery-white metal with an atomic number of 13. It is malleable, ductile, and resistant to corrosion due to the formation of a thin protective oxide layer on its surface.

Aluminium does not occur freely in nature. It is mainly found in bauxite ore and is one of the most abundant elements in Earth’s crust. The metal is extracted through electrolytic processes after refining the ore.

Aluminium is widely used in transportation, construction, and packaging because of its low density and strength. It is also used in electrical transmission, kitchen utensils, and consumer products due to its excellent conductivity and recyclability.

Silicon (Si)

Silicon is a hard, grayish-blue metalloid with an atomic number of 14. It has properties between those of metals and non-metals and is the second most abundant element in Earth’s crust after oxygen.

Silicon does not occur freely in nature. It is mainly found in the form of silicon dioxide and silicate minerals such as quartz, sand, and feldspar. Silicon is produced on a large scale by reducing silica with carbon at high temperatures.

Silicon is best known for its role in the electronics industry as a semiconductor. It is used in computer chips, solar cells, and integrated circuits, and is also important in the manufacture of glass, ceramics, and construction materials.

Phosphorus (P)

Phosphorus is a non-metal with an atomic number of 15 and exists in several allotropic forms, including white, red, and black phosphorus. It is highly reactive and is never found in free form in nature.

Phosphorus is mainly found in phosphate minerals, which are essential components of rocks, soil, and living organisms. It plays a crucial role in biological systems as a part of DNA, RNA, and energy-carrying molecules like ATP.

Phosphorus is widely used in fertilizers to support plant growth. It is also used in the manufacture of detergents, matches, and pesticides, as well as in various industrial and chemical processes.

Sulphur (S)

Sulphur is a non-metallic element with an atomic number of 16. It is usually found as a yellow, brittle solid and has a distinct odor when burned. Sulphur is essential for all living organisms as it is a component of certain amino acids and vitamins.

Sulphur does not occur freely in large amounts, but it is commonly found in minerals such as gypsum, pyrite, and in volcanic deposits. It can also be obtained from petroleum and natural gas.

Sulphur is widely used in the manufacture of fertilizers, sulfuric acid, and chemicals. It is also used in the vulcanization of rubber, production of matches, insecticides, and pharmaceuticals.

Chlorine (Cl)

Chlorine is a yellow-green, highly reactive halogen with an atomic number of 17. It exists as a diatomic gas under normal conditions and has a strong, pungent odor. Chlorine is toxic in its pure form but widely used in controlled chemical applications.

Chlorine does not occur freely in nature. It is mainly found in compounds such as sodium chloride (common salt) and other chlorides in seawater and minerals. It is produced industrially through the electrolysis of brine.

Chlorine is widely used in water purification, disinfectants, and the production of PVC plastics and other chemicals. It is also used in bleaching, pharmaceuticals, and various industrial processes.

Argon (Ar)

Argon is a colorless, odorless, and chemically inert noble gas with an atomic number of 18. It exists as a monoatomic gas under normal conditions and makes up about 1% of Earth’s atmosphere.

Argon is formed through the radioactive decay of potassium in the Earth’s crust and is relatively rare compared to nitrogen and oxygen. It does not readily form compounds due to its stable electron configuration.

Argon is widely used in welding and other high-temperature industrial processes as an inert shielding gas. It is also used in incandescent and fluorescent light bulbs, in double-pane windows for insulation, and in scientific research applications.

Potassium (K)

Potassium is a soft, silvery-white alkali metal with an atomic number of 19. It is highly reactive, especially with water, and must be stored under oil or in an inert atmosphere to prevent rapid oxidation.

Potassium does not occur freely in nature due to its high reactivity. It is mainly found in minerals such as sylvite, carnallite, and in seawater. Potassium is an essential element for living organisms, playing a key role in nerve function, muscle contraction, and fluid balance.

Potassium is widely used in fertilizers to support plant growth, in the manufacture of potassium hydroxide and other chemicals, and in some industrial processes such as glass and soap production.

Calcium (Ca)

Calcium is a soft, silvery-white alkaline earth metal with an atomic number of 20. It is moderately reactive and forms a protective oxide layer when exposed to air.

Calcium does not occur freely in nature due to its reactivity. It is commonly found in minerals such as limestone, gypsum, and fluorite, and is an essential element in bones, teeth, and shells of living organisms.

Calcium is widely used in the production of cement, steel, and alloys. It is also used in agriculture to improve soil quality, in the manufacture of chemicals, and as a dietary supplement for human and animal health.

Scandium (Sc)

Scandium is a silvery-white transition metal with an atomic number of 21. It is relatively soft, lightweight, and has a high melting point. Scandium is not very abundant and is often classified as a rare earth element.

Scandium does not occur freely in nature. It is mainly found in rare minerals such as thortveitite and in small amounts in ores of other metals. The element is produced as a by-product of refining uranium or rare earth metals.

Scandium is used in aerospace components and sports equipment due to its strength-to-weight ratio. It is also used in high-intensity metal-halide lamps and in certain aluminum alloys to improve performance.

Titanium (Ti)

Titanium is a strong, lustrous transition metal with an atomic number of 22. It is lightweight, corrosion-resistant, and has a high melting point, making it valuable for structural and industrial applications.

Titanium does not occur freely in nature due to its reactivity. It is primarily found in minerals such as ilmenite and rutile. Titanium is relatively abundant in Earth’s crust but must be extracted through complex refining processes.

Titanium is widely used in aerospace and military applications, medical implants, and chemical processing equipment. It is also used in pigments, jewelry, and sports equipment due to its strength and resistance to corrosion.

Vanadium (V)

Vanadium is a hard, silvery-gray transition metal with an atomic number of 23. It is known for its corrosion resistance, high strength, and ability to form various alloys.

Vanadium does not occur freely in nature. It is mainly found in minerals such as vanadinite, patronite, and in some iron ores. It is usually obtained as a by-product from the processing of these ores.

Vanadium is widely used in the production of strong steel alloys for tools, construction, and aerospace applications. It is also used in catalysts for chemical reactions, in batteries, and in some specialty glasses.

Chromium (Cr)

Chromium is a hard, steel-gray transition metal with an atomic number of 24. It is highly lustrous, corrosion-resistant, and has a high melting point, making it valuable in alloys and industrial applications.

Chromium does not occur freely in nature. It is mainly found in the mineral chromite and is extracted for use in metal production and chemical industries.

Chromium is widely used in stainless steel and other alloys to improve strength and corrosion resistance. It is also used in plating, pigments, dyes, and refractory materials.

Manganese (Mn)

Manganese is a hard, grayish-white transition metal with an atomic number of 25. It is brittle, moderately reactive, and an important component in many metal alloys.

Manganese does not occur freely in nature. It is mainly found in minerals such as pyrolusite and rhodochrosite. The element is produced through the extraction of these ores.

Manganese is widely used in steel production to improve strength, hardness, and wear resistance. It is also used in batteries, chemicals, and fertilizers, as well as in certain alloys and industrial processes.

Iron (Fe)

Iron is a strong, silvery-gray transition metal with an atomic number of 26. It is malleable, ductile, and magnetic, and it is one of the most widely used metals in the world.

Iron does not occur freely in nature due to its reactivity. It is mainly found in minerals such as hematite, magnetite, and taconite. Iron is produced by smelting these ores in blast furnaces.

Iron is widely used in construction, manufacturing of steel, machinery, and tools. It is also important biologically, as it is a key component of hemoglobin in blood, which carries oxygen in living organisms.

Cobalt (Co)

Cobalt is a hard, lustrous, grayish-blue transition metal with an atomic number of 27. It is magnetic and has good corrosion resistance, making it valuable in various alloys.

Cobalt does not occur freely in nature. It is mainly found in minerals such as cobaltite, erythrite, and skutterudite, often alongside nickel and copper ores. It is extracted through refining these ores.

Cobalt is widely used in the production of superalloys for turbines and aerospace applications. It is also used in rechargeable batteries, magnets, catalysts, and in blue pigments for glass and ceramics.

Nickel (Ni)

Nickel is a silvery-white, hard, and malleable transition metal with an atomic number of 28. It is corrosion-resistant and has good strength, making it suitable for various industrial uses.

Nickel does not occur freely in nature. It is mainly found in minerals such as pentlandite, garnierite, and laterite ores. Nickel is extracted through smelting and refining of these ores.

Nickel is widely used in stainless steel and other alloys to improve durability and resistance to corrosion. It is also used in coins, batteries, plating, catalysts, and rechargeable batteries for electronics and electric vehicles.

Copper (Cu)

Copper is a reddish-brown transition metal with an atomic number of 29. It is highly ductile, malleable, and an excellent conductor of heat and electricity.

Copper does not occur freely in large amounts in nature. It is mainly found in minerals such as chalcopyrite, malachite, and cuprite. Historically, native copper deposits were also mined directly.

Copper is widely used in electrical wiring, plumbing, and electronics due to its conductivity. It is also used in alloys such as bronze and brass, in roofing and plumbing, and for industrial machinery and decorative purposes.

Zinc (Zn)

Zinc is a bluish-white, moderately reactive transition metal with an atomic number of 30. It is brittle at room temperature but becomes malleable when heated.

Zinc does not occur freely in nature. It is mainly found in minerals such as sphalerite, smithsonite, and zincite. It is extracted from these ores through roasting and electrolysis.

Zinc is widely used for galvanizing iron and steel to prevent corrosion. It is also used in alloys such as brass, in batteries, paints, and chemicals, and as an essential trace element for human and animal health.

Gallium (Ga)

Gallium is a soft, silvery-blue post-transition metal with an atomic number of 31. It has a low melting point of about 29.8°C, so it can melt in your hand.

Gallium does not occur freely in nature. It is found in trace amounts in bauxite and zinc ores and is usually extracted as a by-product of aluminum and zinc production.

Gallium is widely used in electronics, especially in semiconductors, LEDs, and solar panels. It is also used in thermometers, high-temperature alloys, and certain medical applications.

Germanium (Ge)

Germanium is a grayish-white metalloid with an atomic number of 32. It is brittle, lustrous, and a semiconductor, making it valuable in electronic applications.

Germanium does not occur freely in nature. It is mainly found in minerals such as germanite and argyrodite, and as a trace element in zinc ores. It is usually obtained as a by-product of zinc and copper refining.

Germanium is widely used in semiconductors, fiber optics, infrared optics, and solar cells. It is also used in LEDs, transistors, and various high-tech electronic and optical devices.

Arsenic (As)

Arsenic is a gray metalloid with an atomic number of 33. It is brittle, has a metallic luster, and is toxic in its elemental form and many of its compounds.

Arsenic does not occur freely in nature. It is commonly found in minerals such as arsenopyrite and realgar, often associated with metals like copper and lead. It is usually extracted as a by-product of mining these metals.

Arsenic is used in semiconductors, alloys, and wood preservatives. Historically, it was also used in pesticides and medicinal compounds, though its toxic nature limits many applications today.

Selenium (Se)

Selenium is a non-metal with an atomic number of 34. It exists in several allotropic forms, including gray, red, and black selenium, and has properties similar to sulfur and tellurium.

Selenium does not occur freely in nature. It is mainly found in metal sulfide ores, often as a by-product of copper refining. It is relatively rare in the Earth’s crust.

Selenium is widely used in electronics, photocopiers, and solar cells due to its photoconductive properties. It is also used in glass production, pigments, and as an essential trace element in nutrition.

Bromine (Br)

Bromine is a reddish-brown halogen with an atomic number of 35. It is a dense, volatile liquid at room temperature with a strong, unpleasant odor.

Bromine does not occur freely in nature. It is mainly found in compounds such as bromides in seawater and certain minerals like carnallite and bromo-gypsum. It is extracted by treating these compounds with chlorine or electrolysis.

Bromine is widely used in flame retardants, pesticides, dyes, and pharmaceuticals. It is also used in water treatment, photography chemicals, and certain industrial processes.

Krypton (Kr)

Krypton is a colorless, odorless, and chemically inert noble gas with an atomic number of 36. It exists as a monoatomic gas under normal conditions and is very rare in Earth's atmosphere.

Krypton is formed in stars through nuclear fusion processes. On Earth, it is found in trace amounts in the atmosphere and is obtained by the fractional distillation of liquid air.

Krypton is used in specialized lighting, such as high-intensity discharge lamps and flash photography. It is also used in some types of lasers, insulation in double-pane windows, and scientific research due to its chemical inertness.

Rubidium (Rb)

Rubidium is a soft, silvery-white alkali metal with an atomic number of 37. It is highly reactive, especially with water, and must be stored under oil or in an inert atmosphere to prevent rapid oxidation.

Rubidium does not occur freely in nature. It is mainly found in minerals such as lepidolite and pollucite, and as a trace element in potassium-rich ores. It is extracted using chemical reduction techniques.

Rubidium is used in research in atomic clocks, electronics, and specialized chemical applications. It is also used in vacuum tubes and in certain types of ion engines and specialty glasses.

Strontium (Sr)

Strontium is a soft, silvery-white alkaline earth metal with an atomic number of 38. It is highly reactive, especially with water, and forms a protective oxide layer when exposed to air.

Strontium does not occur freely in nature. It is mainly found in minerals such as celestite and strontianite. It is extracted from these minerals through chemical processes.

Strontium is widely used in producing red fireworks, ferrite magnets, and in refining zinc. Radioactive isotopes of strontium are also used in medical and scientific applications, including cancer treatment and radiography.

Yttrium (Y)

Yttrium is a silvery-metallic transition metal with an atomic number of 39. It is relatively stable in air, moderately soft, and often classified with the rare earth elements.

Yttrium does not occur freely in nature. It is mainly found in minerals such as xenotime and yttria, often alongside other rare earth elements. It is extracted through chemical separation from these minerals.

Yttrium is widely used in LEDs, phosphors for color television screens, and superconductors. It is also used in alloys to improve strength, in lasers, and in certain medical applications.

Zirconium (Zr)

Zirconium is a lustrous, gray-white transition metal with an atomic number of 40. It is strong, corrosion-resistant, and has a high melting point, making it valuable in industrial and nuclear applications.

Zirconium does not occur freely in nature. It is mainly found in the mineral zircon and is extracted through chemical processes. It is relatively abundant in the Earth’s crust compared to other transition metals.

Zirconium is widely used in nuclear reactors due to its low neutron absorption. It is also used in ceramics, refractory materials, surgical instruments, and alloys to improve corrosion resistance and strength.

Niobium (Nb)

Niobium is a soft, grayish-white transition metal with an atomic number of 41. It is ductile, corrosion-resistant, and has a high melting point, making it useful in high-strength alloys.

Niobium does not occur freely in nature. It is mainly found in minerals such as columbite and pyrochlore, and is extracted through chemical and metallurgical processes.

Niobium is widely used in the production of stainless steel and superalloys for aerospace, pipelines, and electronics. It is also used in superconducting magnets, jewelry, and certain chemical applications.

Molybdenum (Mo)

Molybdenum is a silvery-gray transition metal with an atomic number of 42. It is hard, ductile, and has a very high melting point, making it valuable in high-temperature and corrosion-resistant applications.

Molybdenum does not occur freely in nature. It is mainly found in minerals such as molybdenite and wulfenite, and is extracted through roasting and chemical reduction processes.

Molybdenum is widely used in steel alloys to improve strength, hardness, and resistance to corrosion and wear. It is also used in electrical contacts, lubricants, and catalysts in the chemical industry.

Technetium (Tc)

Technetium is a silvery-gray transition metal with an atomic number of 43. It is radioactive and does not have any stable isotopes, which makes it unique among the lighter elements.

Technetium does not occur freely in nature in significant amounts. It is mainly produced artificially in nuclear reactors or as a by-product of molybdenum decay. Trace amounts can be found in uranium ores.

Technetium is primarily used in medical diagnostic imaging, particularly in radioactive tracers for nuclear medicine. It is also used in research and in certain specialized industrial applications.

Ruthenium (Ru)

Ruthenium is a hard, silvery-white transition metal with an atomic number of 44. It is corrosion-resistant, belongs to the platinum group, and is relatively rare.

Ruthenium does not occur freely in nature. It is mainly found in ores of platinum and nickel, such as pentlandite, and is obtained as a by-product of platinum and nickel refining.

Ruthenium is widely used in electrical contacts, thick-film resistors, and catalysts. It is also used in alloys with platinum and palladium to improve hardness and corrosion resistance, as well as in jewelry and chemical applications.

Rhodium (Rh)

Rhodium is a silvery-white, hard, and corrosion-resistant transition metal with an atomic number of 45. It belongs to the platinum group metals and is highly reflective and rare.

Rhodium does not occur freely in nature. It is mainly found in ores of platinum and nickel, such as pentlandite, and is extracted as a by-product during the refining of these metals.

Rhodium is widely used in catalytic converters for automobiles to reduce harmful emissions. It is also used in jewelry plating, mirrors, electrical contacts, and chemical catalysts due to its stability and corrosion resistance.

Palladium (Pd)

Palladium is a lustrous, silvery-white transition metal with an atomic number of 46. It is soft, corrosion-resistant, and belongs to the platinum group of metals.

Palladium does not occur freely in nature. It is mainly found in ores of platinum and nickel, often alongside other platinum group metals, and is extracted as a by-product of refining these metals.

Palladium is widely used in catalytic converters, electronics, jewelry, and fuel cells. It is also used in dentistry, hydrogen storage, and various chemical reactions as a catalyst.

Silver (Ag)

Silver is a soft, lustrous transition metal with an atomic number of 47. It has the highest electrical and thermal conductivity of all metals and is highly reflective.

Silver does not occur freely in large amounts. It is mainly found in ores such as argentite and in native form. It is often obtained as a by-product of copper, gold, lead, and zinc refining.

Silver is widely used in jewelry, coins, and tableware. It is also important in electronics, photography, solar panels, mirrors, and as a catalyst in certain chemical reactions.

Cadmium (Cd)

Cadmium is a soft, bluish-white transition metal with an atomic number of 48. It is malleable, ductile, and relatively low in melting point, but toxic in nature.

Cadmium does not occur freely in nature. It is mainly found in zinc ores such as sphalerite and is extracted as a by-product during the refining of zinc, lead, and copper.

Cadmium is used in rechargeable nickel-cadmium batteries, pigments, coatings, and in some nuclear reactors. Due to its toxicity, its use is carefully controlled in modern applications.

Indium (In)

Indium is a soft, silvery-white post-transition metal with an atomic number of 49. It is malleable, ductile, and has a low melting point, making it easy to shape and work with.

Indium does not occur freely in nature. It is mainly obtained as a by-product from zinc ores, particularly sphalerite, and is relatively rare in the Earth's crust.

Indium is widely used in electronics, especially in the production of indium tin oxide (ITO) for touchscreens, LCDs, and solar panels. It is also used in low-melting alloys, solders, and certain coatings.

Tin (Sn)

Tin is a silvery-white post-transition metal with an atomic number of 50. It is soft, malleable, and resistant to corrosion, which makes it useful in many applications.

Tin does not occur freely in nature. It is mainly found in the mineral cassiterite and is extracted through smelting and refining processes.

Tin is widely used in coatings for steel cans (tin-plating), in soldering alloys, in the production of bronze, and in various chemical and industrial applications.

Antimony (Sb)

Antimony is a brittle, silvery-gray metalloid with an atomic number of 51. It has properties between metals and non-metals and is resistant to corrosion.

Antimony does not occur freely in nature. It is mainly found in the mineral stibnite and is extracted through roasting and chemical reduction processes.

Antimony is widely used in flame retardants, alloys to increase hardness, batteries, and in semiconductors. It is also used in ceramics, glass, and certain pharmaceuticals.

Tellurium (Te)

Tellurium is a brittle, silvery-white metalloid with an atomic number of 52. It has properties similar to both metals and non-metals and is chemically related to selenium and sulfur.

Tellurium does not occur freely in nature. It is mainly found in telluride minerals such as calaverite and sylvanite, often alongside gold and other metals. It is extracted as a by-product of copper and lead refining.

Tellurium is used in alloys to improve machinability, in thermoelectric devices, solar panels, and semiconductors. It is also used in rubber, glass, and chemical applications.

Iodine (I)

Iodine is a dark gray or purple-black halogen with an atomic number of 53. It exists as a solid at room temperature and sublimates to a violet vapor with a distinct odor.

Iodine does not occur freely in nature. It is mainly found in seawater, brine deposits, and certain minerals such as caliche. It is extracted through chemical processing of these sources.

Iodine is widely used in medicine as an antiseptic and in thyroid treatment. It is also used in photography, dyes, disinfectants, and as a nutrient in animal feed and human diet.

Xenon (Xe)

Xenon is a colorless, odorless, and chemically inert noble gas with an atomic number of 54. It exists as a monoatomic gas under normal conditions and is present in trace amounts in the Earth's atmosphere.

Xenon is formed in stars through nuclear fusion and is obtained on Earth by fractional distillation of liquid air. It does not readily form compounds due to its stable electron configuration.
